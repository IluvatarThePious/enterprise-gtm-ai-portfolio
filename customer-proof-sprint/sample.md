# Customer Proof Sprint — fictional demonstration

**Every company, record, and result in this sample is invented for demonstration. Northline and Cedar Works are fictional labels, not clients. This is not evidence of service performance, a customer endorsement, or a real outcome.** The example demonstrates how to present source-backed claims without overstating them. This document contains a two-part story, one proof-slide outline, three seller emails, and the source ledger.

## Customer story — page 1

### Cedar Works reduced quote-draft preparation time during a four-week pilot

**Fictional demonstration.** Two 120-quote batches show average preparation time changing from 45 to 18 minutes. The difference concerns draft preparation; the example does not establish lower payroll costs or faster end-to-end quoting.

### The business question

Cedar Works, a fictional manufacturer, wanted to understand whether its team could spend less time assembling the first draft of a customer quote. That task included gathering request details and preparing a structured draft for an estimator to review. It was one step in a larger commercial process. Technical review, pricing approval, and sending the final quote remained separate activities.

The question for the pilot was deliberately narrow: would a different preparation workflow reduce the recorded staff time associated with that first draft? A useful answer needed more than a description of the technology. It needed a defined activity, a before-and-after comparison, and clear limits on what the numbers could explain.

### What changed

In this fictional example, Northline supplied a workflow that organized incoming request details into a draft template. An estimator still reviewed the draft and approved pricing before anything could be sent to a customer. The workflow did not authorize quotes on its own, replace the estimator's judgment, or remove the existing approval step.

The supplied pilot note describes the process but does not provide an error-rate study. That matters because faster preparation and better accuracy are separate claims. A sales team using this story could describe the change in recorded preparation time; it could not also claim fewer errors without additional evidence.

### How the comparison was made

The demonstration includes two aggregate time logs. The baseline batch contains 120 quotes with 5,400 recorded preparation minutes. The four-week pilot batch also contains 120 quotes, with 2,160 recorded minutes. Dividing each total by its quote count produces arithmetic averages of 45 and 18 minutes. These are averages, not medians or a promise about every individual quote.

## Customer story — page 2

### What the numbers support

The difference between the two averages is 27 minutes per quote. Relative to the 45-minute baseline average, that is a 60% reduction in recorded draft-preparation time. Across equal batches of 120 quotes, the difference between 5,400 and 2,160 minutes is 3,240 minutes, or 54 hours.

Those 54 hours describe the difference in the two recorded batch totals. They do not establish that the company reduced paid hours, eliminated a position, or converted all of the available capacity into revenue. No wage records, staffing changes, realized savings, or subsequent sales results are included in the fictional source pack. The story therefore keeps the result in units of time.

### Where the result stops

This comparison is observational. The source material does not establish that the two batches had identical complexity, that staff experience remained constant, or that no other process changed. The result is consistent with improved preparation efficiency during the pilot, but the available records do not isolate how much of the difference was caused by Northline alone.

The logs also end at draft preparation. They do not measure total quote turnaround, the proportion of quotes accepted, final accuracy, customer satisfaction, or retention. Extending the headline to any of those outcomes would require additional evidence. Keeping these boundaries visible makes it easier for an evaluator to decide whether the result is relevant to their own process.

### How another buyer could evaluate the workflow

An operations leader considering a similar approach would first define the same preparation task in their own team. They could record quote volume, staff minutes, job complexity, and corrections before and during a pilot, while preserving human review. Measuring downstream approval time separately would help reveal whether a faster draft changes the complete customer experience.

The next decision is whether to run that controlled comparison, not whether to assume a 60% improvement in a different setting. This fictional case demonstrates a practical sales asset: a specific problem, a visible workflow change, a reproducible calculation, and an honest account of the questions still open.

## Proof slide — landscape

### Less time preparing the draft. Human review stays in place.

**Fictional Cedar Works / Northline pilot — illustrative data only**

| Baseline batch | Four-week pilot batch | Observed difference |
|---|---|---|
| 45 min average preparation | 18 min average preparation | 60% lower average |
| 120 quotes / 5,400 min | 120 quotes / 2,160 min | 54 fewer hours across equal-volume batches |

**Workflow:** request details → structured draft → estimator review → pricing approval.

**Buyer implication:** investigate preparation capacity while preserving approval controls.

**Boundary:** observational comparison; quote complexity and other changes not controlled. No measured payroll saving, accuracy improvement, or end-to-end turnaround claim.

**Next step:** compare your own task-level time, complexity, and corrections before and during a pilot.

## Three seller emails

These emails are fictional examples a vendor might use after approving the underlying customer story. They are not outreach from Salesrobots and must not be sent as claims about a real customer.

### 1. To an operations evaluator

Subject: Measuring time spent on quote drafts

You mentioned that assembling a quote draft takes staff time before the estimator can review it. In this fictional example, two 120-quote batches averaged 45 and 18 preparation minutes, with human review retained. The comparison does not measure the full quoting cycle or control for every difference between batches.

Would a small pilot measuring that same task, plus corrections and quote complexity, help you evaluate the workflow in your own team?

### 2. For a champion to forward internally

Subject: A focused quoting-workflow pilot to consider

The attached fictional example describes a workflow that structures request details before estimator review. Recorded preparation time differs by 54 hours between two equal-volume batches of 120 quotes. That is a task-level comparison, not a claim of payroll savings.

For our evaluation, I suggest defining the preparation task, retaining pricing approval, and measuring time, complexity, and corrections before and during a small pilot. That would help us decide whether the workflow fits our operation.

### 3. To a budget owner

Subject: Evidence needed before claiming a financial return

The fictional case shows lower recorded quote-draft preparation time. It does not provide wage, staffing, revenue, or implementation-cost data, so it cannot establish a financial return.

Before considering a wider rollout, we could measure the time difference in your team, include all implementation and operating costs, and identify how any released capacity would actually be used. Would you prefer that evaluation framed around service capacity, turnaround, or another business priority?
